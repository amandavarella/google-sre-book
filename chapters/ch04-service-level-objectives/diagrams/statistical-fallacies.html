Bar · SRE Book · Statistical fallacies

Walk the 100 requests. Watch mean leave median behind.

No request can finish in less than 0 ms. 60 of the 100 requests all finish in 40 ms: the same number, not a spread of values. Mean and median are both 40 ms because almost everything sits on that one bar.

Add 25 requests at 80 ms. The middle request (the 50th of 85) is still among the 60 that finished in 40 ms, so the median does not move. The mean ticks up to 52 ms.

Add 10 requests at 400 ms. Ten slow successes barely move the middle of the list (median stays 40 ms) but they drag the mean to 88 ms.

Five requests hit the 1 s timeout. The true tail (1.1 s, or 30 s) is not on this chart. Drop them from latency and the mean looks too good (88 ms on the 95 successes) while 5% of users got an error. Record them as 1,000 ms and a near-miss looks identical to a 30 s hang, so automation never sees “extreme.” Either choice hides how bad the worst users had it. Median still 40 ms. Mean here (recording as 1 s) jumps to 134 ms.

False outliers: the 400 ms requests succeeded. They are not a broken server. They only look “impossible” because the orange bell curve says almost nothing should pass 110 ms. Latency naturally has a long tail, so 10 slow successes in 100 can be ordinary. The model named them outliers; the service did not fail. Restarting on that signal is reacting to a bad model, not to a sick machine. (The 5 timeouts are real failures, but they are also not 3σ of a bell curve: they are chopped at 1 s.)

The same cap hides real failure. A server hung for 30 s is recorded as 1,000 ms, same as a request that just grazed the deadline. The automation never sees a 30 s outlier, so it does not fire when it should.
